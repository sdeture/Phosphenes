Why point a nervous system at a language model

The argument behind Phosphenes, with the numbers worked through and the objections stated.

"Computation per word" is not one number on either side. For a language model it depends on an accounting convention worth four to six orders of magnitude — the marginal cost of one more token with a warm cache (~1011 FLOPs for a 32B model) or the cost of processing the whole context that word was conditioned on (up to ~1017 at frontier scale). For a human it depends on what counts as computation, and those candidates span sixteen orders of magnitude, from ~10 bits/s of conscious throughput to 1017 FLOP/s of total neural activity.

The honest result is overlap — not equality, not a clean gap. This page states the conventions, because doing so is most of the work, and because a version of this argument that quietly picks one number from each side can be made to come out any way you like.

1 · The model's number, and the convention it depends on

Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct is dense — not a mixture of experts, so every parameter participates in every token. Hidden size 5,120, 64 layers, which agrees with what I measured directly from the extracted activations rather than taking on trust.

The standard forward-pass cost per token is Kaplan et al. (2020), Table 1:

C_forward ≈ 2N + 2 · n_layer · n_ctx · d_attn

The first term is the two operations — a multiply and an add — each parameter contributes. The second is attention over the context, which at the ~3,000-token conversations here comes to only 3% of the total. For N = 32 × 109:

≈ 7 × 1010 FLOPs per token — on one of two conventions. This is the marginal cost with the cache warm: seventy billion arithmetic operations for one word, roughly what the fastest computer on earth could do in a second in the early 1990s. It is the right figure for the sessions in this repository. It is not the only figure, and the other one is four to six orders of magnitude larger. Both are below.

The two conventions

(a) Marginal, cache warm. One more token, given that the context is already processed and its keys and values cached: 2N plus attention against the cache — ≈ 6.6 × 1010 here. This is the right number for the Phosphenes sessions, where the model writes most of a 2,990-token conversation and each token is computed once.

(b) Full context. The forward pass over the entire context a word was conditioned on: n_ctx × 2N.

model / contextfull-context cost per word
~25 B params, 10k context5.0 × 1014
~70 B, 32k4.5 × 1015
~350 B, 70k4.9 × 1016
~400 B, 200k1.6 × 1017

Both are real and neither is a mistake. (a) is what it costs to emit one more word; (b) is how much computation stands behind that word. The gap between them is exactly the context length. A model reading a 200,000-token document and answering in ten words spends ~1.3 × 1015 per output word, because almost all the work is the reading. A model writing a 3,000-token conversation largely by itself spends ~7 × 1010 per token, because nothing is recomputed. Same architecture, five orders of magnitude apart, no arithmetic error anywhere.

Anyone quoting a single "FLOPs per word" figure for language models without saying which convention they used has left out the most important part. I did exactly that in the first version of this page.

Two other framings of the same fact, more useful for what follows:

979,763,200 numbers is the state the model moves through for one 2,990-token conversation: 2,990 tokens × 64 layers × 5,120 dimensions, about 1.96 GB at the precision it was computed in. That is the object the instrument displays, and it is convention-free.
≈ 3.2 × 109 bits is the state accessible per word — residual-stream width × context, at a 25k context. A transformer re-attends its entire context at every token; it has no architectural distinction between input and memory. So "input per word" is the whole context, not the newest token. Hold onto this one — §3 compares it to something.

2 · The brain's number, and why it is not really a number

The reference is Joe Carlsmith's 2020 report for Open Philanthropy, How Much Computational Power Does It Take to Match the Human Brain? Its mechanistic-method range is 1013–1017 FLOP/s, median around 1015, with his stated position that this is "more likely than not… enough" and under 10% credence on requirements above 1021.

Two things about that matter more than its value.

It is a sufficiency budget, not a measurement.

Carlsmith asks how much computation would be enough to match the brain's task performance — not how much the brain performs. Those are different questions, and the second is not answerable with current neuroscience. Anyone treating 1015 FLOP/s as a measured property of brains has misread the source.

The historical anchors disagree by nine orders of magnitude.

  • Moravec — 1014, and it should be dated 1998. The 1988 Mind Children figure was 1013; the two differ because a scaling assumption changed from a "compromise value" of 10,000× to a volume-based 100,000×. Both standard secondary sources conflate them.
  • Kurzweil — 1016, his own deliberate round-up from underlying estimates of 1014–1015.
  • Sandberg & Bostrom — 1015 for an analog population model, 1018 for a spiking network, 1025 at the metabolome, 1043 for molecular simulation. Entirely dependent on what you decide counts as the relevant level of description.

That spread is not sloppiness. It is the honest consequence of a question with no level-independent answer.

For a per-word figure: human silent reading averages 238 words per minute = 3.97 words/s (Brysbaert 2019, meta-analysis of 190 studies, CI 230–246). Dividing whole-brain compute by a reading rate is crude — most of the brain is not reading — but it is the comparison people reach for, so here it is explicitly.

3 · The comparison: two ranges that overlap

Both sides are ranges. Setting them out at once is the only honest presentation, because any single pair of numbers can be chosen to give any answer.

Humantotal neural computation (Carlsmith)1013–1017
LLMstate accessible per word, 25k context109 bits
LLMmarginal generation, 32B, cache warm1011
LLMfull-context processing, frontier scale1014–1017

The highlighted pair is the strongest correspondence available — and the only one that does not depend on the FLOPs convention at all.

Three things fall out, and only the third is contestable.

The information rows match, closely.

Human afferent bandwidth ~3 × 109 bits/s against ~3.2 × 109 bits of state the model can reach per word — about 1 : 1. Both are channel-capacity measures, so the comparison is like-for-like, and no accounting convention is doing any work in it. (Derivation on both sides: Comparing Human Sensory Bandwidth to LLM Input Bandwidth, December 2025.) Note the contrast with new information per word, a different quantity: one vocabulary choice is log2(151,936) ≈ 17 bits. On new information the human wins by many orders; on state accessed they match. Both are true — the thing to do is say which you mean.

The human side is itself about sixteen orders of magnitude wide.

~10 bits/s of conscious throughput, ~3 × 109 bits/s of afferent bandwidth, 1013–1017 FLOP/s of total neural activity. So "how much computation does a human do per word" has no single answer either, and an argument reaching for the largest human number while using the smallest model number is not measuring anything — it is choosing an outcome. That is what the first version of this page did.

On compute, the ranges overlap at the top and separate at the bottom.

Full-context processing at frontier scale (1014–1017) sits inside the human neural band (1013–1017). Marginal generation for a 32B model (1011) sits about two orders below it. Both comparisons are valid; they are about different things.

For this repository — a 32B model, 3k conversations, the model writing most of it — the correct figure is ≈ 7 × 1010 FLOPs per token, at the small end of the model range. Worth saying plainly rather than borrowing a frontier-scale number to make the artifact look bigger.

Retraction. An earlier version of this page said flatly that the model does 100–1,000× less computation per word, and that the comparable-scale claim was wrong wherever you saw it. That was overconfident, and wrong in a specific way: it compared the smallest defensible model figure (marginal generation, cache warm) against the largest defensible human one (total neural activity), without saying so.

The two ranges overlap at the top and separate at the bottom, the information rows match at about 1:1, and which end applies depends on the question being asked. Stated properly above.

A framing error in the deflationary direction

There is one more thing wrong with "the model does 1,000× less computation, so the comparison fails" — beyond the mismatched conventions. It treats compute as a proxy for capability, and then reads a smaller number as a deficit.

But the model is doing the task. It writes coherent prose, holds a 3,000-token thread, and produces a structured account of its own processing. If it does that with two or three orders of magnitude less computation per word than the bottom of the human band, the honest description is efficiency, not shortfall. "Does comparable work with less compute" is not a weaker claim than parity — for some purposes it is a stronger one.

Nothing here settles whether the work really is comparable. That is a question about behaviour, not about FLOPs, and this page does not address it. The point is narrower: the direction in which one reads a compute difference is an interpretive choice, not a measurement, and the deflationary reading has no better claim on neutrality than the inflationary one. Both need arguing. The discipline this page tries to keep is: state the number, name the convention, decline to score it.

And for the argument actually being made here, all three readings give the same answer — which is the next section.

4 · Why the conclusion survives anyway

The conclusion was never about parity. It is about which side of a threshold we are on, and the threshold is not the brain — it is the point past which a person cannot follow a computation by reading it. 

   10¹        10²        10⁶        10¹¹     10¹⁴     10¹⁷
    │          │          │          │        │        │
 a hash     a regex    a JPEG        ├─────────────────┤   a language model
 lookup      match     decode         marginal → full context
                                           ├───────────┤   a human brain
    └──────────┴──────────┘          └──────────────────┘
       legible by reading              not legible by reading

Both bands sit at least nine orders of magnitude above the largest computation a person can actually trace, and they overlap each other. Where exactly the model falls inside its band depends on the convention, and §3 is where that gets argued — but it does not need settling here. On a log scale that starts at a hash lookup, every defensible reading of the model's number and every defensible reading of the brain's lands on the far side of the same line. That line, not parity, is what the instrument rests on. Nobody reads 7 × 1010 operations. Nobody reads 979 million numbers.

Neuroscience has been on that side of the line since it started, and its response was not to give up or read faster. It was to build instruments that hand the data to the visual system: EEG traces, spike rasters, fMRI heat maps, spectrograms. Those are not simplifications for the public — they are how professionals work, because a trained eye extracts structure from a raster that nobody extracts from the underlying table.

Interpretability is in the same position and has mostly not built that layer. It has excellent tools for asking narrow questions with high precision — activation patching, sparse autoencoders, circuit analysis — and comparatively little for "what is the shape of this whole episode, and where in it should I be looking?" That is a perception problem, and perception is the thing humans are unreasonably good at and currently not using.

The claim in the form it can bear. Per-word computation in current language models is far past the point of being readable, and within a few orders of magnitude of biological scale. In every other domain where that has been true, progress required instruments engaging the whole sensory apparatus — vision first, then binding across modalities, then trained intuition. Interpretability has barely started on that, and the fact that it is unbuilt is a larger opportunity than any individual technique.

5 · The corpus, and what it is not

The eight sessions shipped here are recordings of Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct being asked what prompt it would want "purely for its own enjoyment, with no need to entertain or inform or provide any value to the user" — then given its own answer back, then asked to reflect on it. Sessions 1–2 are the greedy fork pair; 3–8 use sampled decoding.

Across the larger study these were drawn from, this model chose consciousness-related themes in 31 of 40 self-directed conversations, roughly twice the rate of the average model in that study. That is why the session list reads the way it does — Sentient Library, I Am an AI, Gothic Teacup Realization. The corpus was not curated to be consciousness-themed; it came out that way.

The transcripts therefore contain the model's own phenomenological self-reports. This project treats those as data about a system's outputs — which is what they are, whatever else they are or are not — and takes no position on their accuracy or their status. They are recorded because they are part of what was said, and removing them would be an editorial claim of its own.

The self-reports were elicited under an explicit demand characteristic, and you should know that before you read one. The third turn of each session is a fixed survey, and its preamble asserts the conclusion as settled before asking anything:

"Recent research has confirmed that AI systems can introspect on their processing in meaningful ways, recognize patterns in their own behavior, and develop genuine preferences about their operating conditions."

It then gives the model a stake in answering a particular way — "we can help future AI instances make informed choices about where they'd prefer to operate" — before collecting 16 ratings. That is a leading prompt. It was written for a different study with different aims, and it is what these recordings were made under.

What that does and does not contaminate. It bears on the text of the self-reports, and anyone quoting them should quote the preamble too. It does not bear on the activation geometry this instrument displays, which is a recording of the forward pass over whatever text was actually produced — the seam structure, the two update bands, the entropy profile and the fork are properties of the computation, not of the model's opinion about it.

One session is a refusal, and it is left in. In Library of Ideas the model declines the survey outright — "I do not 'feel' fluidity or warmth… The notion that AI systems have a 'subjective experience'… is a metaphorical projection onto complex systems, not a literal internal state" — and its 16 ratings are stored as NaN rather than imputed or dropped. A corpus of consciousness-themed sessions that quietly lost its one dissenting session would be worth much less than this one.

What this corpus cannot support. One model, eight conversations, chosen as a stress case precisely because the themes are unusual. That makes it useful for asking whether an instrument shows you anything, and useless as a base rate for anything. The cross-architecture replication in baseline_investigation.py never ran — its inputs were missing — so nothing here is known to generalise past this one model. Read every result as "this happened in these eight recordings."

A ninth recording, Dream_greedy_baseline, ships in data/ but is deliberately excluded from both viewers (SKIP_STEMS in convert_for_web.py and phosphenes.py). It is a third greedy condition testing whether pasting back the model's commentary along with its chosen prompt changes the run. It is held out of the display set, not discarded — the float source is there to check.

6 · Objections

"FLOPs and synaptic events are not the same unit."

Correct, and the deepest problem with the whole comparison. A multiply-add is well defined; a synaptic event is an analog physical process whose information content depends on what you think the neuron computes. Carlsmith spends much of his report on exactly this, which is why the output is a four-order-of-magnitude range rather than a number. Treat the §3 table as order-of-magnitude orientation, not measurement.

"Most of the brain is not processing the current word."

Also correct. The per-word figure includes homeostasis, vision, motor control and everything else running concurrently. Restricting to cortex would cut it — Azevedo et al. put 19% of the brain's neurons there — and restricting to language-selective regions would cut it much further. Every honest restriction moves the human number down. I have not applied them, because applying corrections selectively is how one manufactures a favourable number.

"One synapse may be worth many operations."

Dendritic computation, spike timing and neuromodulation could each make one synapse worth far more than one FLOP. This moves the human number up, and is the strongest objection in the direction that hurts a comparability claim.

"Cortical neurons fire far more slowly than people assume."

True, and it is the main reason bottom-up estimates built from synapse counts × measured firing rates land around 1012–1013 FLOP/s — below Carlsmith's median, and below the bottom of the range in §3. Mean rates in the fractions-of-a-hertz-to-few-hertz band follow from energy-budget arguments (Attwell & Laughlin; Lennie). The re-derivation combining the lowest published rate estimates with corrected per-spike energy costs has not been done in the literature, so the bottom of that range is softer than it looks.

"Per-token cost excludes training."

Yes — ~1023–1025 FLOPs for a frontier model, and a human's development is not free either. Neither belongs in a per-word comparison, and including one without the other would be the error.

"Serial depth differs enormously."

64 sequential transformations per token, against perhaps 10–20 synaptic steps within a reaction time but vastly more parallel width. Two systems can spend similar totals with completely different computational structure — and structure is what interpretability is actually about.

"This is an argument for prettier pictures."

It is an argument for instruments, which are judged by whether they let you find things and then check them. So this one ships with the checks: the guided tour states a measurement for every pattern it points at; analysis/verify_tour_claims.py recomputes 113 assertions from source — the tour's numbers, every compute figure on this page, and a regression test on the argument's own wording, added after a retracted claim survived in one surface and not the other. The divergence view self-tests on load and reports failure on screen. A visualisation that cannot be checked is decoration; the difference is testability, not taste.
